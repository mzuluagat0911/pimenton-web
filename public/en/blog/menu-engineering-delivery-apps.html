Menu Engineering for Delivery Apps: Design a Menu That Sells and Protects Your Margin

Menu engineering for delivery apps is the practice of designing your in-app menu by scoring each item on two axes: conversion (how often people who see it order it) and contribution margin (what's left after food cost, packaging and the app commission). Instead of arranging dishes by taste or tradition, you place, photograph and describe them to steer orders toward items that both sell and pay. A better-ordered menu can lift contribution without spending a cent on ads — because your menu is your real salesperson inside the app.

Your menu inside Rappi, Uber Eats, PedidosYa, DoorDash or DiDi Food is not a decorative card — it's your top salesperson, working 24/7. Every position, photo and description is a margin decision, not an aesthetic one. Yet most restaurants upload the same menu they use in the dining room and wonder why they get lots of views but few orders. Menu engineering fixes that by treating the menu as a system you can measure and optimize.

What menu engineering for delivery apps really means

Menu engineering is the process of classifying each item by conversion and contribution margin, then designing the menu to push demand toward the items that sell and pay. The classic matrix uses four quadrants, adapted here for the delivery channel:

  • Stars — high conversion, high contribution. Your heroes. Protect and feature them.
  • Plowhorses (workhorses) — high conversion, low contribution. People love them but they're thin. Re-engineer cost, add modifiers, or bundle.
  • Puzzles — low conversion, high contribution. Great margin, nobody finds them. Fix photo, description, and placement.
  • Dogs — low conversion, low contribution. Candidates to cut or rework.

The key difference versus a dine-in menu: your contribution math must subtract the app commission and packaging. A dish with a 70% dine-in margin can look very different once you remove a 20–30% take rate and the cost of the container. Classify with the real channel P&L, not the kitchen's gross margin.

How to order your menu so it sells

In-app behavior is top-down and scan-based. People decide fast. Use that:

  1. Put your Stars and Puzzles in the first screen. The first category and the first 2–3 items get the most eyeballs. Don't waste that real estate on drinks or sides.
  2. Lead categories with your highest-contribution sellers, not your cheapest item. The first price a customer sees anchors their expectation.
  3. Keep categories tight. For most restaurants, 5–7 categories is plenty. Too many forces scrolling and decision fatigue; too few hides variety.
  4. Name categories for the customer, not the kitchen. "Best sellers", "Combos for two", "Burgers" beat internal labels.

A practical rule: the top of your menu should answer "what do you recommend?" with your best conversion-times-margin items.

Why you get views but not orders

High menu views and low orders usually means friction or weak persuasion, not weak demand. Common causes: no photos on key items, vague descriptions, prices that feel high without context, a confusing category order, or a checkout with unexpected fees and long delivery estimates. The traffic is already there — you're losing it at the decision moment.

Photos and descriptions: your conversion levers

Photos are the single biggest conversion lever on delivery apps. Platforms and multiple industry studies consistently report that items with clear, appetizing photos sell meaningfully more than those without — often cited in the range of 20–30% uplift, sometimes higher for hero items. The exact number varies by category, but the direction is reliable: a photographed item beats an unphotographed one.

Practical photo rules:

  • One dish per photo, well-lit, filling the frame — no cluttered trays.
  • Shoot the item as it actually arrives, so expectation matches delivery (protects your rating).
  • Prioritize photos for Stars and Puzzles first; you don't need to photograph everything at once.

A good delivery description sells the experience and reduces doubt in one or two lines. It should name the key ingredients, hint at portion size, and remove uncertainty (spice level, what's included). Skip generic adjectives like "delicious". Compare:

  • Weak: "Classic cheeseburger."
  • Strong: "180g smashed beef patty, double cheddar, house sauce and pickles on a toasted brioche bun. Comes with fries."

Combos, upsell and modifiers: raising ticket without discounts

You can lift average ticket without cutting price or running promos — by guiding the order size.

Combos work when they raise contribution per order, not just revenue. The trap: a combo that discounts so hard it undercuts your margin. Build combos around a Star plus a high-margin side or drink, and price the bundle so the total contribution beats selling the main alone. Quick example in round numbers:

  • Burger sold solo: price $10, contribution after food cost, packaging and ~25% commission ≈ $3.50.
  • Burger + fries + soda combo: price $15, added items are high-margin, combo contribution ≈ $5.80.

Same customer, same single order — about $2.30 more contribution with no extra ad spend. Multiply by hundreds of orders and the P&L impact is real.

Modifiers and extras are your highest-margin upsell. "Add bacon", "extra cheese", "make it a large", "add a dip" — these carry low food cost and high perceived value. A well-placed set of optional extras can lift average ticket several percentage points. Keep them optional and relevant; don't force choices that slow the order.

Shorter or longer menu?

For delivery, shorter usually converts better. A tighter menu reduces decision fatigue, speeds the order, and concentrates volume on items your kitchen executes well and fast — which protects prep times and ratings. Long menus bury your best items and create operational drag. Cut the Dogs, keep the proven sellers.

Reduce checkout friction

The last meters matter. Friction that kills conversion includes surprise fees, unclear minimum order, slow delivery estimates, and out-of-stock items left visible. You don't control every platform setting, but you do control item availability, modifier clarity, realistic prep times, and pricing that absorbs the channel cost cleanly. Mark sold-out items off, keep modifier menus short, and make sure your menu prices already account for commission so the final total doesn't shock the customer.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Classifying items by kitchen margin instead of post-commission contribution.
  • Ordering the menu by habit, putting low-margin or low-interest items first.
  • Launching combos that discount into negative contribution.
  • No photos on your highest-contribution items.
  • A giant menu that hides your Stars and slows the kitchen.
  • Blaming the app for weak sales before auditing the menu — the first fix is almost always the menu itself.

Menu engineering is the cheapest growth lever in delivery: no media budget, just better decisions about what you show and how. Measure conversion and contribution per item, redesign around the matrix, and let your menu do the selling.

Frequently asked questions

In what order should I show products on a delivery app?

Lead with your Stars and Puzzles — the high-contribution items with strong or improvable conversion — in the first category and first few slots, since those get the most views. Order each category starting with your best conversion-times-margin item, not the cheapest. The first screen should answer "what do you recommend?".

How many categories should my app menu have?

For most restaurants, 5–7 customer-friendly categories work best. Too many forces endless scrolling and decision fatigue, while too few hides your range. Name them for the customer ("Best sellers", "Combos for two") rather than internal kitchen labels.

Do photos increase sales on delivery apps, and by how much?

Yes — photos are the single biggest conversion lever in-app. Industry and platform data commonly cite uplifts around 20–30% for items with clear, appetizing photos versus none, with hero items sometimes higher. Prioritize photos for your highest-contribution sellers first.

How do I raise average ticket without lowering prices or running promos?

Use combos built around a Star plus high-margin sides or drinks, and optional modifiers like extra cheese, bacon, or size upgrades. These carry low food cost and high perceived value, so they lift contribution per order without discounting. Keep extras relevant and optional so they don't slow checkout.

Why do I get many menu views but few orders?

High views with low orders usually signals persuasion or friction problems, not weak demand. Common causes are missing photos, vague descriptions, confusing menu order, prices without context, or a checkout with surprise fees and long delivery estimates. The traffic is there — fix the decision moment before blaming the channel.
