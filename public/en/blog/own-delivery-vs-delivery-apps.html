Own Delivery vs Delivery Apps: How to Decide With Numbers

Own delivery is not automatically cheaper than delivery apps. Apps charge a commission but bring discovery and demand; your own channel saves that commission but absorbs customer acquisition cost (CAC), riders and support. For most restaurants the answer is a hybrid model: use apps to acquire new customers and your own channel to retain repeat buyers, measuring contribution per order on each.

The classic debate — own delivery versus delivery apps — is almost always framed wrong. Operators compare one number (app commission) against another (cost per drop) and conclude the own channel is cheaper. That comparison ignores the most expensive line item of all: who generates the order in the first place. Before deciding how much weight to give each channel, you need to count every cost layer, not just the obvious one.

Why the comparison is usually wrong

When you put a Rappi, PedidosYa, Uber Eats or DoorDash commission next to the price of hiring a rider, the app looks expensive and the own channel looks free. It isn't. The app commission is a bundled price: it includes demand, discovery, the payment rails, the delivery network and customer support. Your own channel unbundles all of that — which means you now pay for each piece separately, and some of those pieces are harder to see on the P&L.

The honest way to decide is to break delivery economics into three cost layers and compare them channel by channel.

The three cost layers of any delivery order

1. Demand and CAC

This is the layer nobody sums. An app order arrives with demand already attached — the customer found you inside a marketplace with millions of users. On your own channel, you have to generate that demand: Meta and Google ads, SEO, promos, your own database. That is a customer acquisition cost (CAC), and for a first-time buyer it can easily rival or exceed an app commission.

2. Logistics

This is delivery itself: riders, fuel or fleet contracts, dispatch, insurance, idle time between orders. On apps, logistics is usually included in the take rate. On your own channel you either hire riders or contract a last-mile provider — and the real cost of in-house riders includes wages during slow hours, not just the minutes they spend delivering.

3. Operation and support

Order management, the ordering platform or website, payment processing fees, customer service, refunds and complaints. Apps absorb most of this. Run it yourself and every late order, wrong item or failed payment becomes your team's phone call.

Only when you price all three layers per channel can you compare apples to apples. A common mistake is counting layer 2 for own delivery and comparing it against the full app commission — which quietly hides layers 1 and 3.

What each channel is actually good at

Framed this way, the two channels stop being rivals and start doing different jobs:

  • Delivery apps are an acquisition and discovery engine. They bring incremental demand — customers who would not have ordered from you directly. You pay a commission, but part of that is marketing you didn't have to run.
  • Own delivery is a retention and margin engine. It shines with customers who already know you and would come back anyway. Here you skip the commission, so the saved points go to contribution — as long as CAC stays low because the demand is repeat, not bought.

The strategic question isn't "which is cheaper," it's: for this specific order, who created the demand? New demand usually belongs on apps. Repeat demand is where an own channel pays.

A volume rule to decide the mix

There's no universal order count where own delivery "wins," because it depends on density and repeat rate. But the logic is consistent:

  1. Low volume or spread-out zones: apps almost always win. You can't keep a rider busy, so your cost per own drop explodes and CAC is high because few customers repeat.
  2. High volume in a tight radius with strong repeat: the own channel starts to make sense. Riders stay busy, cost per drop falls, and a loyal base lowers CAC toward near-zero.
  3. Everything in between — most restaurants: the answer is a hybrid model, not a binary choice.

The practical test: estimate your orders per rider-hour inside a deliverable radius. If a rider can complete enough drops per hour to beat the app's effective take rate — after you've added CAC and support — your own channel is viable for that zone and that customer segment. If not, apps are doing the heavy lifting for a reason.

The default answer: a measured hybrid

For most multi-location restaurants the winning setup is not "leave the apps" or "only apps." It's using each channel for the job it does best:

  • Apps to acquire: let marketplaces bring new customers and incremental demand you couldn't reach alone.
  • Own channel to retain: move the repeat buyer — who already chose you — to your direct channel, where you keep the commission.
  • Measure contribution per channel: track contribution margin per order on apps and on own delivery separately. Don't manage them as one blended number, or you'll never see which channel is funding which.

Migrating repeat customers to your own channel is a retention play, not a rebellion against apps: inserts in the bag, loyalty perks, a faster direct checkout, better pricing on repeat orders. You keep the apps for what they're best at — discovery — and build margin on the customers who already love you.

The mistake that costs the most

The biggest risk isn't picking the wrong channel — it's deciding without numbers. Launching own delivery to "save commission" and ignoring CAC, idle rider hours and support costs can leave you with lower margin than the app you were trying to avoid. The villain is never the platform; it's running the channel blind. Measure all three cost layers, compare contribution per order, and let the numbers set the mix.

Frequently asked questions

Is own delivery cheaper than delivery apps?

Not automatically. Own delivery saves the app commission but adds customer acquisition cost, riders, idle hours and support. It's only cheaper when you have enough repeat volume in a tight radius to keep riders busy and keep CAC low.

How much does it really cost to run in-house riders?

More than the per-drop cost. Real cost includes wages during slow hours, fuel or fleet, insurance, dispatch and idle time between orders. The key metric is orders per rider-hour: low density makes each own delivery far more expensive than it looks.

At what order volume does an own channel make sense?

There's no universal number — it depends on delivery density and repeat rate. Own delivery starts to pay when you have high volume in a tight radius with strong repeat customers, so riders stay busy and acquisition cost approaches zero. Below that, apps usually win.

Can I run apps and my own channel at the same time?

Yes, and for most restaurants that hybrid model is the recommended default. Use apps to acquire new customers and incremental demand, and your own channel to retain repeat buyers where you keep the commission. Measure contribution per channel separately.

Does own delivery create new demand or just move existing demand?

On its own it mostly moves demand you already have — repeat customers who would have ordered anyway. Apps bring incremental, discoverable demand. That's why a hybrid works: apps generate new customers, the own channel retains them at better margin.
